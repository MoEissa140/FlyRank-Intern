Abstract

Organizations managing large content libraries face a recurring operational question: which pages warrant editorial attention first? This study evaluates whether observable search and engagement signals, measured over a trailing ninety-day window, can rank content by decline risk more effectively than a single rule-based heuristic. Using a mid-panel month (March 2026) drawn from FlyRank's pseudonymized search warehouse, five features — mean impressions, clicks, position, engagement rate, and scroll rate — were used to predict whether a page's search visibility would decrease over the subsequent month. A logistic regression model, validated under a client-grouped split designed to prevent cross-client memorization, achieved an AUC of 0.539 and a Precision@20 of 0.65, a modest but measurable improvement over a threshold-based baseline (AUC 0.495, effectively equivalent to chance). Feature-importance analysis indicates the model relies almost entirely on position and impressions; engagement and scroll signals contributed negligibly, a result consistent with an independent finding that engagement rate did not scale with session volume in the direction conventionally assumed. These results are presented as a weak, directional decision-support signal suitable for prioritizing manual review — not as a reliable predictive instrument, and not as evidence that any specific intervention will reverse an observed decline.

Introduction

FlyRank's portfolio-wide research report, The State of AI-Driven SEO (April 2026), documents that content health typically peaks sixty to ninety days after publication and declines steadily thereafter, and that refreshing sufficiently mature pages can produce substantial recovery in measured visibility. That finding motivates a practical question the original report does not resolve at the individual-page level: within a library of hundreds of thousands of pages, which specific pages should a reviewer examine first, given finite editorial capacity?

This study frames that question as a ranking problem rather than a causal one. The objective is not to establish that refreshing content causes recovery — the source report's own methodology explicitly cautions against reading its correlational patterns as causal claims — but to construct a decision-support tool that orders pages by evidence of movement, and to test that tool against a simple, fully transparent baseline a team could implement without any modeling infrastructure at all.

This paper makes two specific, testable claims, and no others:

  1. C1. Under a client-grouped validation split, logistic regression achieves a higher AUC and Precision@20 than a single-threshold baseline rule on identical data (§5).
  2. C2. The validation design itself is load-bearing: a naive random split overstates performance relative to a client-grouped split by a measurable margin, demonstrating that client identity, not just feature signal, was partially available to the naive model (§5).

No claim is made about causal impact of any recommended action, about performance on data outside the March–April 2026 window, or about reliability for clients with limited tracking history.

Exclusions

FlyRank's product-level decision outputs — health_score, priority_score, and action_type — are absent from this release entirely, a fact confirmed through direct schema inspection rather than assumed from documentation. Had these fields been present, they would have been excluded from the feature set regardless: incorporating a product's own decision output as a predictive feature risks the model reproducing that decision rather than discovering independent signal, a failure mode referred to here as a circular result.

All identifiers (client_hash_id, content_hash_id) are pseudonymized by FlyRank prior to release. No raw client names, URLs, domains, or query text appear in this analysis, in the underlying notebooks, or in the repository accompanying this paper.

Methodology

Label definition

A content item is labeled as declining if its mean daily impressions in the April target window fall below its mean daily impressions in the March feature window. This is a directional proxy label rather than a magnitude-aware forward-looking outcome, and it is treated accordingly throughout the analysis: no claim in this paper interprets it as more than an observed change in direction.

Baseline

The baseline is a single, fully interpretable rule: a page is flagged declining_with_demand if its April impressions fall below its March impressions and its April impressions remain at or above one hundred, a floor intended to exclude noise from very low-traffic pages. This rule constitutes the benchmark that any model must exceed under identical data and identical validation to justify the additional complexity.

Validation design

Two validation designs were compared directly to quantify the risk of overfitting to client-specific patterns. A naive random split (eighty-twenty) permits pages belonging to the same client to appear in both the training and test partitions, which allows a model to partially memorize client-level traffic norms rather than learn signal that generalizes across clients. A client-grouped five-fold split (GroupKFold on client_hash_id) instead constrains every test fold to contain only clients absent from that fold's training data. Unless otherwise noted, all results reported in this paper use the client-grouped design.

Leakage audit

A deliberate leakage test preceded finalization of the feature set: a column derived directly from the target window (forward-month impressions) was temporarily added to the model, producing a sharp and implausible increase in AUC, then removed. The feature set reported here contains no field calculated from the April window, no FlyRank product-decision flag, and no field not independently confirmed to be available before the March-to-April decision point.

Results

Random-selection expectation for this label — the rate at which a page is labeled declining with no model at all — is 0.635 (63.5 percent) in the development sample. This is a materially imbalanced label: nearly two in three pages are tagged declining under this definition. This matters for interpretation: a naive strategy of flagging every page would already achieve 63.5 percent precision by construction, purely from the label's skew. Read against that reference point, the baseline rule's AUC of 0.495 reflects genuinely poor discrimination — worse than uninformative — while the modest AUC gains from logistic regression and random forest indicate the models are extracting a real, if weak, signal beyond the label's imbalance. Precision@20 is less directly comparable to this base rate, since it reflects rank-ordering quality among the top candidates rather than raw classification accuracy.

Precision@20 requires the base rate to interpret correctly. With 63.5 percent of the development sample labeled declining, a random top-20 selection would be expected to score approximately 0.635 by chance alone. Logistic regression's observed 0.65 is therefore only marginally above what random selection would produce at this operating point, and should not be read as strong discriminative performance. AUC, which is rank-based and insensitive to base rate, remains the more informative metric in this table: logistic regression's 0.539 against the baseline's 0.495 reflects a real, if modest, improvement over chance-level ranking (AUC 0.5).

Logistic regression was the strongest performer on this feature set, exceeding both the baseline rule and the random forest model. That a more flexible ensemble method underperformed a linear model suggests the relationship between these five features and the decline label is closer to monotonic than to a complex non-linear interaction; with only five features and a binary label, the additional flexibility of the random forest appears to introduce variance rather than capture additional structure.

The 0.078 gap between the naive and grouped splits corresponds to the memorization effect anticipated in the methodology: a portion of the naive split's apparent performance reflects the model's recognition of which client a page belongs to, rather than a signal that generalizes to a client it has not previously scored. The grouped estimate is reported as the primary result throughout this paper.

Average position (0.52) and average impressions (0.35) jointly account for approximately eighty-seven percent of the model's decisions. Average clicks (0.06), scroll rate (0.05), and engagement rate (0.02) contribute comparatively little. This pattern is consistent with an independent signal-audit finding from this project: pages with higher session volume did not exhibit higher engagement rates, contrary to the conventional assumption, which may partly explain the limited predictive weight assigned to engagement here.

Limitations and Honest Framing

This is a pattern study, not a causal study. No result presented here demonstrates that refreshing a flagged page causes recovery in search visibility — only that pages sharing certain observed characteristics have historically moved in certain directions.
  • Weak absolute discrimination. An honest AUC of 0.51 to 0.54 is only modestly better than chance. The ranked queue should be treated as a coarse triage aid, not a confident prediction.
  • Sparse engagement coverage. Only 413,966 of 9,841,378 rows in the development month (4.2 percent) contain available GA4 data. Engagement-based reasoning is unreliable for the large majority of the panel, reflecting incomplete tracking coverage rather than an absence of engagement.
  • Single development month. Features and labels were constructed from one mid-panel month (March to April 2026). Seasonality, one-off traffic events, and client-specific publishing cycles are not explicitly controlled for.
  • Proxy label. The declining label reflects a directional impression change rather than a magnitude-aware or persistence-checked definition, and may capture ordinary month-to-month variance rather than a structural decline.
  • Unbalanced client history. Clients vary substantially in available tracking history within the warehouse; predictions may be less reliable for recently onboarded clients with limited history.

Ranked Recommendations

Pages are ranked by the model's out-of-fold predicted decline probability and assigned one of four reason codes, allowing a reviewer to assess at a glance why a given page appears in the queue.

high_risk_visible_page

High predicted decline risk combined with meaningful traffic at stake (at least 500 impressions). Highest review priority.

model_decline_risk

High predicted decline risk with lower current visibility. Warrants review.

page_buried_with_demand

Not flagged as declining, but ranked beyond position twenty with measurable demand (at least 100 impressions) — a visibility opportunity rather than a decline signal.

monitor

No strong signal in either direction. No immediate action indicated.

Required human review before acting on any flagged page

  • Whether the pattern reflects a sustained decline, a short-lived fluctuation, or consolidation of traffic to a related page on the same site.
  • Whether the page belongs to a client with limited GA4 history, in which case engagement-based reasoning behind the flag may be unreliable.
  • Whether a known external factor — seasonality, a site migration, or a competitive shift — better explains the pattern than a content-quality issue.

Processes that should not be automated

  • Publishing content changes on the basis of the model score alone; this queue is designed to prioritize review, not to trigger action directly.
  • Citing predicted_decline_prob as a confidence estimate in external communication; the model's honest AUC is too close to chance to support that framing.
  • Merging or removing pages on the basis of this ranking alone; consolidation decisions require independent human verification in every case.

Monitoring and retraining triggers

  • Re-measured grouped AUC on a subsequent month falls meaningfully below approximately 0.51, indicating the learned pattern may no longer hold.
  • A substantial influx of clients with traffic or GA4-coverage profiles that differ materially from the March 2026 training panel.
  • Schema changes in the underlying warehouse release, which have been observed to occur between build versions; any pipeline reuse should re-verify the schema before assuming column names remain stable.
  • Suggested retraining cadence: monthly, using an identical feature-and-label window structure and re-validated under the client-grouped split on each occasion.

What I Would Test Next

  1. A multi-month backtest. This study evaluates a single feature-to-label window (March→April 2026). Re-running the same pipeline across several consecutive month pairs would show whether the 0.51–0.54 AUC range is stable or whether it varies with seasonality and portfolio composition.
  2. A sealed, out-of-time holdout. All results reported here come from cross-validation within the same development window. A model trained on March data and evaluated once, untouched, on a later calendar month not used during any development would be a stronger test of real generalization than cross-validation alone provides.
  3. A controlled refresh experiment. Nothing in this study establishes that acting on a flagged page changes its trajectory. A small randomized pilot — flagging a page for review versus leaving a comparable page untouched — is the only way to move from a ranking claim to a causal one.
